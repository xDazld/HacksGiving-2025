# Strategic Benchmarking Report: Revenue Optimization, Community Engagement, and Artificial Intelligence Integration for the Milwaukee Mitchell Park Domes

## Executive Summary

The global landscape for botanical gardens and conservatories is undergoing a profound structural transformation. No longer static repositories for plant collections, leading institutions are evolving into dynamic, data-driven cultural hubs that actively participate in the "experience economy." For the Milwaukee Mitchell Park Domes, a historic landmark facing critical decisions regarding its future viability, the operational strategies of its peers offer a clear, evidence-based roadmap.

This report provides an exhaustive analysis of 20 comparable institutions—ranging from the localized community models of the Lincoln Park Conservatory to the global research powerhouses like the New York Botanical Garden. The analysis dissects their financial architectures, community engagement frameworks, and adoption of emerging technologies, specifically Artificial Intelligence (AI).

The findings reveal a stark bifurcation in the sector. Institutions relying on traditional, passive admission models are facing stagnation and capital deferred maintenance crises. Conversely, institutions that have embraced a diversified revenue portfolio—characterized by high-yield seasonal programming, dynamic pricing algorithms, and tiered membership structures—are achieving financial sustainability and robust capital reserves. The analysis of revenue drivers indicates that "blockbuster" events, such as the _Garden Lights, Holiday Nights_ at the Atlanta Botanical Garden and the _Holiday Train Show_ at the New York Botanical Garden, often generate a disproportionate share of annual operating revenue, effectively subsidizing the institution's scientific and educational missions.

Furthermore, the role of the botanical garden in the community has shifted from "attraction" to "essential infrastructure." Institutions like Phipps Conservatory have successfully insulated themselves politically and financially by embedding their mission into the public health fabric of their cities through programs addressing food insecurity and urban wellness. This shift from "nice to have" to "need to have" is a critical strategic pivot for ensuring long-term public support.

Finally, the integration of Artificial Intelligence represents the next frontier for the sector. Far from being a futuristic novelty, AI is currently being deployed to solve existential challenges: digitizing vast herbarium collections to track climate change at the Missouri Botanical Garden, optimizing energy consumption in aging glasshouses at Longwood Gardens through predictive climate control, and enhancing visitor accessibility through AI-driven interpretation tools. This report synthesizes these trends into actionable insights, positioning the Milwaukee Domes to not only survive but to thrive as a leader in the next generation of botanical conservatories.

## Section 1: Financial Architecture and Revenue Diversification

The financial health of modern botanical institutions relies on a "three-legged stool" model: earned revenue (admissions, retail, rentals), contributed revenue (donations, grants, membership), and endowment draws. The analysis of the peer group indicates a decisive trend toward maximizing earned revenue through sophisticated pricing strategies and the monetization of underutilized assets (evening hours, winter seasons).

### 1.1 The Evolution of Admissions: From Static to Dynamic Models

The traditional fixed-price admission model is increasingly viewed as inefficient, failing to capture value during peak demand periods while doing little to stimulate attendance during troughs. Peer institutions are rapidly adopting dynamic and demand-based pricing strategies similar to those used in the airline and hospitality industries.

#### 1.1.1 Dynamic Pricing and Yield Management

The implementation of dynamic pricing allows institutions to adjust ticket prices in real-time based on inventory, weather, and historical demand patterns.

**Atlanta Botanical Garden (ABG):** ABG serves as a primary case study for this approach. For its marquee event, _Garden Lights, Holiday Nights_, ticket prices are not static. They fluctuate based on the date and projected attendance, with peak weekend slots commanding significantly higher prices than weeknights.[^1] This strategy serves a dual purpose: it maximizes revenue yield from tourists and less price-sensitive visitors who prefer weekends, while simultaneously flattening the demand curve by incentivizing price-sensitive locals to visit on less crowded weekdays. This operational smoothing reduces strain on staff and infrastructure while optimizing the visitor experience.[^2]

**Empire State Building Model:** While not a garden, the Empire State Building's adoption of AI-driven dynamic pricing is cited in the industry as a benchmark. Algorithms analyze weather forecasts, local event schedules, and real-time sales velocity to adjust pricing hourly.[^3] Botanical gardens, heavily influenced by weather, are beginning to adopt similar, albeit simplified, versions of this logic to mitigate revenue losses from inclement weather.

#### 1.1.2 The Strategic Use of Timed Ticketing

Post-pandemic, timed ticketing has transitioned from a safety protocol to a permanent revenue optimization tool. It creates a sense of scarcity that drives advance purchasing and allows for precise labor forecasting.

**Longwood Gardens:** The institution mandates timed reservations for members and guests during high-demand periods like _A Longwood Christmas_ and _Spring Blooms_.[^4] This control mechanism prevents overcrowding, protecting the integrity of the visitor experience—a crucial factor in justifying premium ticket prices. By controlling flow, Longwood can also optimize throughput in retail and food service outlets, preventing bottlenecks that discourage spending.

**New York Botanical Garden (NYBG):** Similarly, NYBG employs timed entry for the _Holiday Train Show_, ensuring that the visitor density within the Enid A. Haupt Conservatory remains manageable.[^5] This exclusivity allows them to market the event as a premium experience, vital for maintaining its status as a "must-do" NYC holiday tradition.

#### 1.1.3 The "Accessible" and Hybrid Revenue Models

A subset of the peer group operates on donation-based or hybrid models, often due to municipal charters or mission-driven mandates.

**Lincoln Park Conservatory:** Operating within the Chicago Park District, this conservatory relies on a suggested donation model ($10 for adults, $5 for children) rather than enforced admission.[^6] While this maximizes accessibility, it severely limits earned revenue, forcing the institution to rely heavily on the Lincoln Park Conservancy for capital fundraising and programming support.[^7] The Conservancy's financial filings indicate a net fundraising income of approximately $106,000 in 2022, which must cover gaps left by the lack of ticket revenue.[^8]

**Gardens of Golden Gate Park:** This entity represents a successful hybrid approach. Formed by the merger of the Conservatory of Flowers, Japanese Tea Garden, and San Francisco Botanical Garden, it charges admission to non-residents while remaining free for San Francisco residents.[^9] This strategy effectively "taxes" tourists to subsidize local access. In 2022, this model generated $1.8 million in non-resident admission revenue, a 48% increase year-over-year, demonstrating the viability of segmented pricing based on residency.[^9]

### 1.2 Membership Economics: Value Stacking and Retention

Membership programs are shifting from simple transactional relationships (fee-for-entry) to emotional and experiential commitments. Successful peers are "stacking" value to move members up the giving ladder.

#### 1.2.1 Tiered Experiential Benefits

**Franklin Park Conservatory:** This institution exemplifies the "experiential tier" strategy. Their "Conservatory Club" membership ($240) includes standard admission but adds unique, non-monetary perks such as "first access to purchase annually harvested honey from Conservatory hives" and recognition on a digital donor wall.[^10] These low-cost, high-exclusivity benefits foster a deeper emotional connection with the donor.

**The Huntington:** The Huntington utilizes behavioral economics in its pricing structure. Its "Sustaining" level ($175) is explicitly marketed as "paying for itself in less than 3 visits," anchoring the value proposition in usage while encouraging a higher upfront commitment than a basic level.[^11] Furthermore, their high-level "Society of Fellows" drives significant unrestricted operating support, essential for maintaining their vast collections.[^12]

#### 1.2.2 Digital Transformation and Retention

The shift to digital membership cards is not merely a cost-saving measure; it is a data acquisition strategy.

**Adoption:** Institutions like Atlanta Botanical Garden, Brooklyn Botanic Garden, and Chicago Botanic Garden have moved to digital platforms (e.g., Cuseum, PassPlay).[^13]

**Retention Mechanics:** Digital wallets allow for push notifications regarding renewals and upcoming events, keeping the institution top-of-mind. Predictive analytics tools are now being used to identify "at-risk" members based on visitation patterns (e.g., a member who hasn't visited in 6 months), allowing for targeted re-engagement campaigns before the membership lapses.[^16]

### 1.3 The Blockbuster Event Economy

For many peers, the fiscal year is defined by one or two major seasonal events that generate the surplus required to operate the facility year-round.

#### 1.3.1 The "Light Show" Phenomenon

**Atlanta Botanical Garden:** The _Garden Lights, Holiday Nights_ exhibition is a revenue juggernaut. The garden's total revenue exceeds $35 million, with admissions and the light show comprising the largest share.[^17] The event attracts tourists from surrounding states, expanding the garden's market reach well beyond the metro area.[^18]

**Olbrich Botanical Gardens:** Even smaller, municipally-partnered gardens can leverage this model. Olbrich's _GLEAM_, a light-based art exhibition, drew over 39,000 guests in a single season.[^19] This event serves as a major fundraiser, demonstrating that "night activation" is scalable and not reserved for only the largest institutions.

**Franklin Park Conservatory:** Their _Conservatory Aglow_ extends the visitor season into the winter nights, transforming the outdoor gardens and indoor biomes into a light spectacle. This drives membership sales in Q4, typically a slow period for garden joins.[^20]

#### 1.3.2 Cultural and Artistic Integration

**Red Butte Garden:** This university-affiliated garden is an outlier, deriving approximately 65% of its operating budget from its outdoor concert series.[^22] With a capacity of 3,000, the amphitheater effectively turns the garden into a premier performing arts venue. This model suggests that "botanical" missions can be subsidized effectively by "cultural" entertainment.

**Chihuly Garden and Glass:** This institution represents the total fusion of art and horticulture. With high ticket prices ($35-$42.50) and aggressive bundling with the Space Needle, it operates as a high-yield tourist attraction.[^23] While a commercial entity, its model of integrating permanent, high-value art installations offers a lesson for non-profits on increasing perceived value.

## Section 2: Community Engagement and Social Impact

The most successful peer institutions have redefined their value proposition. They have moved beyond being "nice places to visit" to becoming "essential community infrastructure." This strategic pivot opens access to funding streams related to public health, education, and urban resilience, which are often larger and more sustainable than arts funding.

### 2.1 Public Health and Food Security as Mission Drivers

**Phipps Conservatory (Pittsburgh)** stands as the premier model for this transformation. Under its "human and environmental health" framework, Phipps has launched programs that directly address social determinants of health.

**Homegrown Program:** This initiative installs raised-bed vegetable gardens in underserved neighborhoods (such as Homewood and Larimer) and provides ongoing mentorship to families. To date, they have installed over 400 gardens.[^26] This program moves the institution's mission outside its gates, directly addressing food apartheid. It builds deep trust and political capital within the city, positioning the conservatory as an agent of social equity rather than an elitist enclave.[^27]

**Let's Move Pittsburgh:** Phipps facilitates a collaborative program focused on childhood health and nutrition, convening healthcare providers, educators, and community leaders.[^28] By acting as the backbone organization for this initiative, Phipps accesses public health grants and corporate sponsorships (e.g., UPMC Health Plan) that would not typically fund a flower show.

**Brooklyn Botanic Garden** leverages its "Greenest Block in Brooklyn" contest to mobilize entire neighborhoods. This program creates a competitive yet collaborative framework for urban greening, engaging residents who may not be regular garden visitors but become fierce advocates for the institution because it supports their local beautification efforts.[^29]

### 2.2 Educational Innovation: STEM and Citizen Science

The integration of botanical gardens into formal education systems ensures relevance and recurring visitation.

**Garfield Park Conservatory (Chicago):** The SEED (Student Engagement and Educational Development) program, funded by IMLS, transforms the conservatory into a STEM learning laboratory for Chicago Public Schools. By targeting West Side partner schools specifically, the conservatory aligns its resources with the needs of the district, making it an indispensable educational partner rather than just a field trip destination.[^30]

**Missouri Botanical Garden:** The PlantLab Student Scientists program provides free, downloadable curriculum and loanable tool kits to schools, allowing students to conduct citizen science projects in their own schoolyards.[^31] This "scalable" model allows the garden to impact thousands of students without being constrained by the physical capacity of its on-site classrooms.

**United States Botanic Garden (USBG):** Through its PLACCE (Plants and Climate Change Education) cohort, the USBG connects peer institutions to develop best practices for teaching climate change.[^32] This positions the garden as a national thought leader and a convener of professional expertise.

### 2.3 Inclusivity and Cultural Access

Ensuring the garden reflects its community is both a moral imperative and a business necessity for long-term growth.

**Museums for All:** Franklin Park Conservatory and The Gardens of Golden Gate Park actively participate in this national initiative, offering deeply discounted admission (typically $3 or free) to EBT/SNAP cardholders.[^33] This removes economic barriers and diversifies the socio-economic profile of the visitor base.

**Olbrich Botanical Gardens:** Their educational programming, such as "Garden Naturalist" camps and "Writing Camps," broadens the interpretation of the garden beyond horticulture to include humanities and arts. This interdisciplinary approach attracts segments of the youth population who might not identify as "science kids".[^35]

**Daniel Stowe Conservancy:** Recognizing its limited administrative capacity, this garden entered into a shared services collaboration with the Catawba Riverkeeper. This innovative model allows both non-profits to share back-office costs (finance, HR, facilities) while scaling their combined programming impact on the region's environmental health.[^36]

### 2.4 Volunteerism as Strategic Engagement

Volunteers are not just free labor; they are the institution's most loyal constituency.

**Foellinger-Freimann Botanical Conservatory:** This institution integrates volunteers into critical revenue-generating operations, such as the Garden of Lights (parking, concessions) and the gift shop.[^37] In return, volunteers receive perks like free access to the light show. This symbiotic relationship reduces labor costs during labor-intensive events while deepening the volunteers' emotional investment in the garden's success.

**Lincoln Park Conservancy:** With a reliance on donations, the Conservancy utilizes "EcoStewards" and docents to maintain the park and interpret collections, effectively staffing the facility through community goodwill.[^38]

## Section 3: The AI Revolution in Botanical Institutions

The integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) is reshaping the botanical garden sector across three primary domains: **Visitor Experience**, **Conservation Science**, and **Operational Efficiency**. The "Smart Garden" utilizes data to enhance engagement, protect collections, and optimize resources.

### 3.1 AI for Enhanced Visitor Experience (The "Smart Guide")

Visitor expectations have shifted; static placards are no longer sufficient. The modern visitor demands on-demand, personalized, and interactive information.

#### 3.1.1 Hyper-Personalization and Interactive Storytelling

**Conversational Agents:** Museums are pioneering the use of AI chatbots to facilitate non-linear narratives. For example, the Museum of Tomorrow uses AI to discuss sustainability with visitors, tailoring the conversation to the user's specific lifestyle habits.[^39]

**Interactive Interpretation:** While not a direct botanical peer, the Château de Versailles implementation of "Ask Mona" allows visitors to "chat" with statues via AI-enabled QR codes. This technology is directly transferable to a conservatory setting, where visitors could "text" a rare palm or a desert cactus to learn about its biology, history, or conservation status.[^40]

**Bloomberg Connects:** Major institutions like NYBG, Brooklyn Botanic Garden, and Phipps have adopted the Bloomberg Connects app.[^41] This platform provides a unified digital infrastructure, allowing gardens to deliver deep content (audio tours, maps, botanical data) without the prohibitive cost of developing and maintaining a proprietary app.

#### 3.1.2 AI-Driven Wayfinding and Crowd Management

**Crowd Prediction:** Technologies utilized by high-volume institutions (like the Louvre and increasingly applicable to sites like Longwood) use Bluetooth sensors and historical data to predict congestion. AI models then suggest alternate routes to visitors in real-time.[^44] For the Domes, applying this during peak events like a holiday show could significantly improve the visitor experience by mitigating bottlenecks.

**Multilingual Access:** AI translation tools can instantly translate interpretive materials and audio guides into dozens of languages, breaking down barriers for non-English speaking tourists and diverse local communities.[^39]

### 3.2 AI in Conservation and Horticulture (The "Smart Greenhouse")

For facilities managing controlled climates and vast collections, AI offers transformative operational benefits.

#### 3.2.1 Herbarium Digitization and "Big Data" Botany

**Missouri Botanical Garden (MBG)** and **New York Botanical Garden (NYBG)** are global leaders in the application of AI to botanical science.

**Revolutionizing Species Identification (RSI):** MBG secured a landmark $14.4 million grant to digitize its herbarium using AI. The project uses machine learning algorithms to scan millions of pressed plant specimens, identifying traits and species classifications faster than humanly possible.[^46]

**Climate Resilience:** This digitized data allows scientists to track changes in plant morphology over centuries, providing critical data on how species are adapting (or failing to adapt) to climate change. NYBG uses similar AI models to identify climate-resilient species for restoration projects.[^48] For the Domes, participating in such data networks could elevate its scientific status from a local display garden to a global research node.

#### 3.2.2 Predictive Maintenance and Climate Control

**Longwood Gardens** is pioneering the "Smart Conservatory" in its new West Conservatory project.

**Automated Climate Strategy:** Longwood employs sophisticated sensors that feed data into an AI-driven control system. This system ingests weather forecasts, internal sensor data (humidity, temperature, light levels), and plant physiological requirements to optimize vent openings, shading deployment, and heating systems.[^49]

**Predictive vs. Reactive:** Unlike traditional thermostats that react to temperature changes, these AI systems predict thermal loads based on cloud cover forecasts and solar angles, adjusting the environment preemptively. This results in significant energy savings and reduced stress on the plant collection.[^50]

#### 3.2.3 Robotics in the Canopy

**Atlanta Botanical Garden** has deployed the **SlothBot**, a slow-moving, energy-efficient robot that lingers in the canopy of the Storza Woods. Designed by Georgia Tech engineers, the robot monitors micro-climates, CO2 levels, and weather conditions over long durations.[^52] This engaging application of robotics serves a dual purpose: collecting high-fidelity environmental data and providing a unique "hook" for STEM education and visitor interest.

### 3.3 AI in Business Intelligence and Fundraising

AI is revolutionizing the "back of house" operations, particularly in how gardens identify and retain donors.

**Donor Propensity Modeling:** Tools like DonorSearch AI allow nonprofits to analyze their donor databases against external wealth and behavioral data. The AI predicts which donors are most likely to give, the optimal ask amount, and the best timing for solicitation.[^53] This shifts fundraising from intuition to precision science.

**Dynamic Pricing Algorithms:** As noted in the revenue section, AI models analyze historical sales, weather patterns, and competitive event schedules to set optimal ticket prices.[^3] This maximizes yield during high-demand periods and can automate discounts to drive volume during low-demand times.

## Section 4: In-Depth Case Studies

To provide granular strategic insights, this section examines three peer institutions that offer distinct models applicable to the Milwaukee Domes.

### 4.1 Case Study: Phipps Conservatory (Pittsburgh, PA) – The Sustainability & Wellness Model

**Context:** Phipps operates a historic glasshouse complex similar in era and constraint to the Domes. However, it has successfully reinvented itself as a global leader in sustainability.

**Strategy:** Phipps pivoted its brand identity from "flower shows" to "human and environmental health."

**Programmatic Core:** The Homegrown program[^26] and Let's Move Pittsburgh[^28] position the garden as a public health entity. This attracts funding from health insurers and foundations that typically do not support botanical arts.

**Financials:** Phipps generates approximately $17.5 million in total revenue, with a healthy mix of contributions ($8.8M) and program services ($6.3M).[^56] Their focus on sustainability—exemplified by the "Living Building" certified Center for Sustainable Landscapes—attracts distinct capital funding.

**Lesson for Domes:** Aligning the Domes with public health, food security, and urban sustainability can unlock new, non-traditional funding streams and deepen community relevance beyond the "tourist" demographic.

### 4.2 Case Study: Atlanta Botanical Garden (Atlanta, GA) – The Event-Driven Revenue Model

**Context:** ABG has achieved massive financial growth through aggressive, high-quality event programming.

**Strategy:** Investing heavily in "blockbuster" exhibitions that justify high ticket prices.

**The Light Show:** _Garden Lights, Holiday Nights_ is not merely a display; it is a complex logistics operation featuring dynamic pricing, massive marketing campaigns, and corporate sponsorship.[^1]

**Art Integration:** Exhibitions like "Chihuly in the Garden" turn the garden into an outdoor art museum. This "artification" of the garden allows them to charge premium admission ($40+) and attract cultural tourists who might not visit a garden solely for plants.[^57]

**Expansion:** The success of this model has fueled a $160 million campaign to expand the garden to the Atlanta Beltline, further integrating it into the city's urban fabric.[^58]

**Lesson for Domes:** High-quality, ticketed seasonal events are the most effective mechanism to subsidize year-round operations. The Domes' unique structure is ideally suited for internal projection mapping and light shows (e.g., similar to _Conservatory Aglow_ at Franklin Park[^21]).

### 4.3 Case Study: Missouri Botanical Garden (St. Louis, MO) – The Research & Digital Innovation Model

**Context:** A historic institution managing aging infrastructure (the Climatron) while maintaining a position as a global scientific leader.

**Strategy:** Leveraging intellectual property, data, and scientific expertise.

**AI Leadership:** The "Revolutionizing Species Identification" project secured a $14.4M grant, proving that "back-of-house" science can drive major philanthropic support.[^46]

**Community Integration:** The garden leads the "Deer Creek Watershed Alliance," positioning itself as a leader in regional environmental infrastructure projects.[^59]

**Lesson for Domes:** Partnering with academic institutions (like the prompt's mention of MSOE) to create a "living lab" for AI and conservation can attract substantial federal and private research grants, funding infrastructure upgrades under the banner of "scientific innovation."

## Section 5: Strategic Synthesis and Recommendations for the Milwaukee Domes

Based on the comprehensive analysis of the 20 peer institutions, the following strategic recommendations are proposed to guide the future of the Mitchell Park Domes.

### 5.1 Revenue Diversification Strategy

1. **Implement Dynamic Pricing for Special Events:** The Domes should adopt a dynamic pricing model for high-demand events (e.g., Holiday Shows, Train Shows), similar to the strategies employed by Atlanta and Longwood. This captures maximum revenue from tourists and weekend visitors. To ensure equity, "pay-what-you-wish" or deeply discounted access should be reserved for Milwaukee County residents on specific off-peak days.[^9]

2. **Develop a "Conservatory After Dark" Series:** Modeling after Franklin Park's _Conservatory Aglow_ or Chihuly's evening access, the Domes should monetize the evening hours. The unique geodesic geometry of the Domes makes them ideal canvases for internal projection mapping and light shows. These events are high-yield and have a low impact on the plant collection compared to physical installations.[^21]

3. **Restructure Membership Tiers:** Move beyond simple admission passes. Create a "Conservatory Circle" tier that offers experiential benefits—such as early access to plant sales, behind-the-scenes breeder tours, and digital recognition—inspired by Franklin Park's "Conservatory Club" model.[^10]

### 5.2 Community Engagement and Social Capital

1. **Launch a Neighborhood Greening Initiative:** Replicate Phipps' Homegrown program. Helping residents in the immediate vicinity of the Domes install raised beds or pollinator gardens creates a tangible physical and social link between the institution and the neighborhood.[^26] This builds a local constituency that views the Domes as a partner in their daily lives.

2. **Integrate with School Curricula:** Formalize a STEM partnership similar to Garfield Park's SEED program. Use the distinct biomes (Desert, Tropical) as living classrooms for climate change education, utilizing the PLACCE network framework (Plants and Climate Change Education) utilized by the US Botanic Garden.[^32]

3. **Leverage Volunteer Power:** Adopt the Foellinger-Freimann model of integrating volunteers into revenue-generating roles (concessions, event staffing) to lower the overhead of new event programming.[^37]

### 5.3 The "Smart Domes" AI Strategy

The prompt specifically requests AI solutions. Based on the peer review, the Domes should pursue a "Smart Conservatory" roadmap:

**Phase 1: Visitor Engagement (The "Domes Bot"):** Deploy a generative AI chatbot, trained on the Domes' specific history and botanical collection, accessible via QR codes throughout the facility. This bot could adopt a persona (e.g., "The Time-Traveling Botanist") to guide visitors, answer questions in multiple languages, and provide accessibility descriptions for visually impaired guests.[^39] This is a low-cost, high-impact "quick win" that modernizes the visitor experience without heavy infrastructure.

**Phase 2: Operational AI (Predictive Climate):** Partner with engineering programs (like MSOE) to install IoT sensors in the Domes. Use AI to model thermal dynamics and predict heating needs. This creates a "Digital Twin" of the Domes (similar to Desert Botanical Garden's GIS twin[^60]), allowing for optimized energy usage and predictive maintenance of the aging glass structure. This directly addresses the facility's maintenance challenges.

**Phase 3: Conservation Data:** Use AI to digitize the Domes' plant records and engage the public in "citizen science" data collection. Participating in global initiatives like the Missouri Botanical Garden's digitization efforts can open doors to new grant funding sources.[^46]

## Conclusion

The analysis of 20 peer institutions confirms that the Milwaukee Mitchell Park Domes possess the foundational assets—iconic architecture, distinct biomes, and an urban location—requisite for success. However, the "passive conservatory" model is obsolete. To ensure financial sustainability and community relevance, the Domes must evolve into an active, programmed, and digitally integrated campus.

By adopting the revenue aggression of Atlanta, the community integration of Phipps, and the digital forwardness of Missouri, the Domes can secure their legacy not just as a historic landmark, but as a thriving, future-proof institution. The integration of AI technologies offers a unique opportunity to leapfrog traditional operational constraints, turning the Domes' structural challenges into opportunities for innovation.
